Venezuelan lawyer Clovis Trevino is a partner in Covington & Burling's international arbitration practice in Washington, DC. Her work spans investor-State arbitration, public international law, commercial disputes, award enforcement, and business and human rights. Over the course of her career, she has represented companies, investors, and States in some of the most complex international disputes of our time, including serving as Co-Agent and counsel for Ukraine before the International Court of Justice and representing global trade union federations in landmark business and human rights arbitrations. Less than two weeks after the full-scale invasion of Ukraine began, she appeared before the International Court of Justice as part of Ukraine's legal team. Following her opening keynote address at the sixth MIDS Alumni Association Annual Conference, Clovis reflects on the education, relationships, and experiences that shaped her path from Venezuela to the forefront of international dispute settlement.

body: >-


"The MIDS taught me the value of being open to insights from different fields while remaining precise about the law applicable to each dispute."

Clovis Trevino
(MIDS Class 2012), Partner, Covington & Burling

What motivated you to join the MIDS program, and what were your expectations at the time?

I came to the MIDS because I was fascinated by how disputes crossing borders and legal systems could be resolved through law. Coming from Venezuela, I was particularly interested in developing an international practice and understanding how lawyers from different legal traditions approached the same questions. The breadth of the program appealed to me. It offered the opportunity to study commercial arbitration alongside investment treaties, human rights, and disputes between States. I wanted to understand the framework governing each and develop the skills to work across them. I was particularly interested in how the interpretation of a treaty or contract could shape both the scope of a tribunal's authority and the rights of the parties before it.  My expectations were primarily about learning and preparing for practice. I did not yet know precisely what shape my career would take, and I valued a program that would allow me to explore different interests while building a strong foundation.

Which aspect of the MIDS experience had the greatest impact on your professional or personal growth?
The 

MIDS gave me a framework for understanding different systems of international dispute settlement-from human rights mechanisms and investment treaties to commercial arbitration and disputes between States. Studying them together helped me understand their distinct approaches to jurisdiction, interpretation, procedure, and remedies, as well as the questions they share.  That comparative perspective has stayed with me. In practice, you need to identify the particular legal framework governing a dispute, interpret the relevant treaty or contract, and understand the substantive rights and procedural options available to the client. Having that foundation helps you approach unfamiliar issues with confidence and ask better questions.  The colleagues and faculty had an equally lasting impact. Learning alongside people from different countries and legal traditions challenged my assumptions and broadened my perspective. Our professors brought academic depth and practical experience, connecting the subjects we studied with the decisions lawyers make in practice.


"Studying different systems of international dispute settlement together helped me understand both their distinct approaches and the questions they share."
Clovis Trevino
(MIDS Class 2012), Partner, Covington & Burling

How has the MIDS network or training influenced your career path since graduation?
The  MIDS gave me the legal foundation to develop a broad international disputes practice. It also helped me develop habits that remain essential to my work: identifying the decisive issues, analyzing them carefully, and explaining a position clearly to an international audience.  Experience has taught me to combine that analysis with a close understanding of the client's objectives and, in commercial matters, its business. Alongside the legal merits, clients need advice on the cost, timing, potential recovery, and effect on their wider interests and relationships. Useful advice brings those considerations together and helps the client make a decision.  The MIDS community has also remained valuable. Its alumni and faculty work across firms, businesses, governments, institutions, and academia around the world. That range of perspectives is particularly valuable in a practice that involves collaborating across jurisdictions and understanding different legal and commercial environments.


"You cannot predict every question your career will present, so build a foundation that allows you to keep learning."
Clovis Trevino
(MIDS Class 2012), Partner, Covington & Burling

Can you briefly explain your career path since graduating from the MIDS?

Since the  MIDS, I have developed a global practice spanning investor-State arbitration, public international law, commercial disputes, award enforcement, and business and human rights. I am now a partner at Covington & Burling in Washington, DC, representing companies, investors, and States across regions and legal systems.  Investor-State arbitration is a substantial part of my work. I find the questions of treaty interpretation, consent, investment protection, and State responsibility intellectually engaging. Applying those principles to particular facts also requires a detailed understanding of the investment and its business and regulatory context. My public international law practice includes serving as Co-Agent and counsel for Ukraine before the International Court of Justice. For almost a decade, I have represented Ukraine and Ukrainian companies in a range of international proceedings against the Russian Federation. Each mandate presents distinct questions of jurisdiction, applicable law, and evidence.  Commercial arbitration is also central to my practice. I enjoy learning how a client's business operates, understanding the contractual relationship underlying a dispute, and working with the client to determine what a successful outcome would look like. That means understanding its priorities, appetite for risk, and the commercial consequences of the available options.  I also advise on the recognition and enforcement of arbitral awards across jurisdictions. That work requires close collaboration with local counsel, an understanding of domestic legal frameworks, and practical judgment about the options available to give effect to an award.  In business and human rights, I represented global trade union federations in the first known arbitrations under a business and human rights agreement, brought under the Bangladesh Accord on Fire and Building Safety against global fashion brands. Those proceedings concerned contractual commitments relating to garment factory safety and brought together questions of contract interpretation, international supply chains, and human rights.  The geographical range of my work is part of what I enjoy: collaborating with clients and counsel around the world and understanding the legal and business environments relevant to each dispute. My Venezuelan background informs that international perspective, and I work in both English and Spanish, including in bilingual proceedings.  I value the breadth of my practice and the opportunity to develop a detailed understanding of each matter. My approach is consistent: understand the facts and the client's objectives, develop a clear strategy, and continually assess whether the steps we are taking advance those objectives.

What is a misconception about arbitration or international law that you believe the MIDS helped you see differently?

One misconception is that the different systems of international dispute settlement operate in separate worlds: commercial arbitration concerns contracts, investment arbitration concerns treaties, and human rights belongs to another field altogether. The MIDS helped me see the connections between them, while appreciating the distinct legal framework governing each.  Those connections have become particularly interesting in practice. An investment treaty dispute may require a detailed understanding of contractual rights and domestic law. A business and human rights arbitration may turn on the interpretation of contractual commitments. Recognition and enforcement proceedings bring international awards into contact with national courts and procedural rules.  Understanding those relationships helps you identify questions you might otherwise overlook. It also requires care: concepts developed in one system cannot simply be transferred to another. The MIDS taught me the value of that comparative perspective-being open to insights from different fields while remaining precise about the law applicable to each dispute.


"Subjects I had studied in Geneva: investment arbitration, treaty interpretation, State responsibility, and international adjudication, were now central to my daily responsibilities."

Clovis Trevino
(MIDS Class 2012), Partner, Covington & Burling

Which moment since graduation made you realize the true value of your MIDS experience — and was it what you expected?

The weeks surrounding Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 stand out. I was at the Peace Palace in The Hague, representing Naftogaz in an investment treaty arbitration against Russia, when the invasion began. We had arrived for a hearing-to examine witnesses, present arguments, and answer the tribunal's questions.  Overnight, the circumstances around us changed profoundly. Yet the hearing continued, with counsel for both sides continuing to present their cases. As that hearing drew to a close, we turned quickly to preparing Ukraine's application to the International Court of Justice and its request for urgent provisional measures. I stayed in The Hague, and less than two weeks after the invasion began, I appeared before the Court as part of Ukraine's team.  Those weeks made me appreciate the breadth of my MIDS education in a very immediate way. Subjects I had studied in Geneva-investment arbitration, treaty interpretation, State responsibility, and international adjudication-were now central to my daily responsibilities. The proceedings involved different clients, legal bases, and questions, each requiring careful analysis within its own framework.  I could never have anticipated those circumstances as a student. What stayed with me was the importance of being able to draw on a strong legal foundation while working closely with a team, adapting quickly, and maintaining the care and precision each client's case required.


"Less than two weeks after the invasion began, I appeared before the International Court of Justice as part of Ukraine's team."
Clovis Trevino
(MIDS Class 2012), Partner, Covington & Burling

What advice would you give to future MIDS students embarking on this journey?

Take advantage of the breadth of the program, and learn from classmates whose backgrounds and perspectives differ from your own. You cannot predict every question your career will present, so build a foundation that allows you to keep learning.  For those interested in commercial practice, be curious about business. Understand how companies operate, how contracts allocate risk, and how a dispute affects the people making decisions. Ask what the client needs to achieve and what constraints it faces.  Finally, develop the ability to turn complex analysis into clear advice. Take the intellectual questions seriously: examine the text closely, question your assumptions, and engage with competing interpretations. Clients need your judgment about what matters, what their options are, and how to move forward.
